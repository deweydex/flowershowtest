# Learning happens in your head

Most of the learning in my courses does not happen in the classroom. It happens on your own time, at home, in a library, in a coffee shop, while you work on an assignment, a project, a reflection or a check-in. Group work is welcome and will come up, but the learning itself can only happen in one head at a time, and the evidence of it comes from connecting exactly one brain to the keyboard. So the classroom is not where I deliver information. The information in my courses exists out there already, none of it is new, and none of it is hard to find. The classroom is where we set intentions, try things, get stuck, talk about being stuck, and reflect on what happened.

That is why I take no notes for you and require none from you. Missing something is not a possibility in my courses, because what we are learning is posted publicly and repeatedly. How you learned it matters less than the fact that you learned it.

# Every subject is emotional

Nobody arrives in a maths class, or a programming class, without a history. Many of my students are adults returning to education who were told at some point, in words or otherwise, that they were bad at maths, or not the kind of person who codes. Those habits and expectations come with them, and in my experience they matter more than access to a good teacher or good content. When I ran workshops for tutors at Paper, I learned that we teachers are shaped by our own schooling in ways that are hard to notice and harder to change. The first job in any class, then, is to make it safe to be wrong. A change that breaks your web page is not a failure. It is information about what that line does.

# Learning is making

I ask for a lot of writing: reflections, project logs, check-ins, planning at the start of a class and reflection at the end. None of it is graded for content. Students who do it perform noticeably better on standardised assessments, and succeed more readily in the course as a whole, because writing is where thinking becomes visible to the person doing it. The same goes for code and for mathematics: write the brief in your own words, list the behaviours you want, think through the variables and the conditions, and only then start typing. Explaining an answer is more than showing your work. It is teaching to the page.

# Assessment without punishment

My assessments tend to be project-based or to use online resources, and neither involves a terminal grade after one attempt. There are many opportunities to improve, because the point of an assessment is to show me what you have learned and where you might need something more. Success has to take into account where each learner starts and how far they have come. The more you engage, submit, participate and reflect, the more easily I can see what you have learned, and the more flexibility I can give you.

# Adults, not pupils

My classes are groups of adults, so I assume you can act on your own needs without asking. Nobody needs permission to drink water or take a call outside. What we owe each other instead is mutual respect, clear communication and honesty with ourselves about what we have and have not done. Rules are a poor substitute for norms, and a classroom of adults can build its own.

# Teaching computer science

Most of my experience teaching computer science comes from two sources: managing instructors, and teaching mathematics through programming. The first case involved working with four fantastic instructors at Youth Employment Services in Toronto. Over the course of my time there we completely revamped the programme's structure. In our AWS certification course especially, we focused on a portfolio approach, so that learners had more than just a badge at the end of it: they also had a series of projects that demonstrated what they had learned, beyond the initial course parameters, and that served as evidence to employers that they could do more than answer multiple choice or fill-in-the-blank questions. In the end, we found that those who had completed the portfolio were more than twice as likely to get employed, which was our key metric.

In the second case, in both South LA and Berlin, I used programming as a way of helping high school students gain proficiency in mathematics. My own first steps into computer science were through the lens of mathematics, since that was the field I was in when I first developed an interest in programming. If you want someone to understand a concept, almost any concept, though technical ones translate most directly, have them code it up! With Python offering such a low barrier to entry, I found myself encouraging Google Colab and Jupyter notebooks to make the time between idea and implementation as short as possible. Most students started out skeptical, feeling that the subjects were entirely disparate, but by the end I had a good dozen students able to program with full control flow, object-oriented programming and recursion. That is still how I teach both subjects, and it is why [dewlab](/Current%20Projects) exists.

# Finding out who is in the room

In the first weeks of my classes I spend a good deal of time learning the level, academic background, and emotional connections of my students in reference to the subjects I am teaching. Sometimes this takes the form of an informal poll in class; other times I use a questionnaire, so that I can get a lot of quantitative and qualitative data quickly and easily. Differentiation is the norm rather than the exception, and I cannot differentiate for people I do not know.

The questions are mostly ordinary. What is your programme, what led you to it, what would you like to do afterwards? What are your interests? (I go first: reading, fancy coffee and the cafes that serve it, woodworking, dark chocolate, fresh baked crusty bread, house plants, jazz, hiking, and educational YouTube as a guilty pleasure.) Have you lived anywhere other than Dublin, and do you speak other languages? How was school for you, did you connect with it, did you have any great teachers? Do you have a laptop you can bring? Outside of school, when you have to learn something new, where do you go?

Then the subject-specific ones. For maths: what maths courses have you taken, how did they go, what was difficult and what was fun, and how is maths shown in the films and media you watch? For programming: have you programmed before, in what, have you used an editor or a notebook, what first got you interested, is there something specific you would like to build, have you made a website, and how is programming shown in the media you watch? The answers to that last pair tell me more about the emotional starting point of a class than anything else on the form.

# Where this comes from

My teaching is informed by the progressive education movements of both Europe and North America, and by a few people in particular: the praxis of Paulo Freire, the learner independence of Maria Montessori and John Dewey, the Big Picture Learning schools of Dennis Littky and Elliot Washor, Alfie Kohn on the damage that rewards do, and the late Sir Ken Robinson. My own path ran through a Computer Science and Mathematics degree at McGill, a Master's in Computer Science and Mathematics Education at the Freie Universität Berlin, machine learning at the Technical University of Berlin, and an MSc in Human Centered Artificial Intelligence at Technological University Dublin. The most interesting things always seem to happen when the technical fields mix with the humanistic ones, and that is what I try to teach.
