# Understanding Student AI Use Beyond Simple Detection

## Reframing the Conversation

Much of the discourse around students using AI tools focuses on detection and prohibition. While these concerns are understandable, this framing can obscure more productive questions: *Why* are students turning to these tools, and what does that tell us about how we might better support their learning? A hermeneutic of generosity—an interpretive approach that assumes the best of students rather than the worst—can help us understand student choices more fully and respond more pedagogically.

---

## A Hermeneutic of Generosity Toward Students

When we approach student AI use with generosity, we assume that students are generally trying to learn and succeed, not trying to cheat the system. This doesn’t mean ignoring academic integrity, but it does mean starting from curiosity rather than suspicion. What barriers might this student be facing? What needs are they trying to meet? What skills might they be missing?

This generous approach reveals that “AI use” isn’t a single behavior with a single motivation. Different students use these tools for different reasons, often reflecting different struggles. Understanding these contexts helps us respond more effectively.

---

## Three Contexts for AI Use

When a student submits work generated or heavily assisted by an AI, the underlying situation often falls into one of three categories. Each suggests different pedagogical responses, and each invites us to consider what the student might actually need from us.

### 1. Struggles with Language

Some students have a solid grasp of course material but struggle to express their understanding in writing. This includes students for whom the language of instruction is not their first language, students with writing-related learning differences, and students who simply lack confidence in their written expression.

**What this might look like:** The student can explain concepts verbally but their written work is disorganized or unclear. AI-assisted submissions show understanding of content but in a voice or style that feels unfamiliar. The student may express frustration that they “know it but can’t say it.”

**A generous interpretation:** This student is trying to demonstrate knowledge they actually possess but lack the linguistic tools to express adequately. The AI becomes a translation aid—not from one language to another, but from internal understanding to external expression.

**Questions to consider:** Are we assessing their understanding of content or their writing ability? If both, are we making that distinction explicit? Are there ways to scaffold the writing process that help students develop expression skills rather than bypassing them? Would alternative assessment formats (oral presentations, annotated diagrams, multimedia) allow them to demonstrate mastery while also developing communication skills?

### 2. Struggles with Material

Other students may be genuinely lost with the course content. They turn to AI not to polish their expression but because they do not know where to begin. The AI becomes a way to produce something—anything—when they feel they have nothing.

**What this might look like:** The student seems disengaged or overwhelmed in class. AI-assisted work may contain errors that suggest the student did not understand what was generated. They may be unable to answer follow-up questions about their submission or explain their reasoning.

**A generous interpretation:** This student is drowning and grasping for anything that might keep them afloat. Using AI to produce an assignment may feel less shameful than submitting nothing or revealing the full extent of their confusion. They may not even understand what help they need.

**Questions to consider:** What earlier concepts might they be missing? Are there prerequisite skills we assumed but did not verify? How might we create lower-stakes opportunities to identify gaps earlier, before students are too far behind to recover? Could the AI itself be used as a tutoring tool rather than a production tool—explaining concepts rather than generating answers? What support structures might help this student catch up rather than fall further behind?

### 3. Struggles with Time Management or Competing Demands

Some students understand the material and can write effectively, but are juggling competing demands or have not prioritized the work. AI becomes a shortcut when time runs out or when other commitments take precedence.

**What this might look like:** The student demonstrates competence on in-class work but submits AI-generated content for longer assignments. There may be patterns across multiple courses. Their AI-assisted work is polished but impersonal. The student may acknowledge using AI if asked directly.

**A generous interpretation:** This student is making strategic choices about how to allocate limited time and energy across multiple competing demands. They may be working full-time, caring for family members, managing health issues, or dealing with other life circumstances that we may not know about. Using AI may feel like a survival strategy, not a moral failing.

**Questions to consider:** Are assignment deadlines and workloads realistic given students’ other commitments? Are there structural barriers (work schedules, caregiving responsibilities, financial pressures) we should know about? How might we help students develop planning and time management skills without shaming them for their current strategies? Is the assignment design itself creating perverse incentives—rewarding completion over learning?

---

## Moving Beyond Detection

Detection tools are unreliable and often produce false positives, particularly for non-native speakers. More importantly, a purely punitive approach misses an opportunity. If we treat AI use only as a rule violation, we lose the chance to understand what students need and how we might teach more effectively.

A hermeneutic of generosity doesn’t mean abandoning academic integrity policies. Rather, it means complementing those policies with curiosity about the *why* behind student choices, and a willingness to respond pedagogically as well as administratively. It means assuming that most students, most of the time, are trying to succeed rather than trying to deceive us.

---

## Practical Implications

- Consider whether your assessments distinguish between content knowledge and expression skills

- Build in process checkpoints (drafts, outlines, reflections) rather than relying solely on final products

- Create opportunities for students to demonstrate understanding in multiple formats

- When AI use is suspected, start with questions rather than accusations

- Discuss AI tools explicitly in your course, including their limitations and appropriate uses

- Recognize that students facing multiple barriers may need support, not punishment

- Design assignments where the process of learning is visible, not just the product
