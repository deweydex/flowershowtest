# Teaching Materials: Click on the Titles!

My teaching and content (and management) styles are informed by progressive education movements in both a European and an American context, though they are also influenced heavily by some truly amazing pedagogues. I owe a great debt to the praxis-oriented methods of Paulo Freire, to ideas around learner independence and self-determination as described by Maria Montessori and John Dewey, and to more modern educational giants such as the creators of Big Picture Learning (Dennis Littky and Elliot Washor), the late but great Sir Ken Robinson, and a few others who I will include in the resources and recommendations section.

Below are a few examples of my work from my time teaching. Unfortunately, the vast majority of the work I produced for organisations such as Nelson and Paper remains their property, and I am not able to share it here. What follows are a few exercises, learning materials and workshops I have produced, along with some context surrounding their creation or delivery. Since some of them are several years old now, they are not exactly a depiction of my current pedagogical preferences, but they offer a way to see how I used to teach, which certainly informs my current practice.

# [Measuring the World: an Introduction to Modeling and Scientific Notation (8th–10th Grade)](https://drive.google.com/drive/folders/1dibQOpgwlpSpJU7l-uR6bGy7D0wUMHWe?usp=sharing)

This series was delivered to a fantastic 8th-grade bilingual Math class under the mentorship of David Brown. It served as part of my teacher-practicum evaluation at the Nelson Mandela School, during the Computer Science and Mathematics Education Masters Program at the Freie Universität Berlin. These kids had a blast. My evaluator initially thought there was no way to have 8th graders learn the material this way, but by the end the students showed that often the biggest barriers are those placed in front of learners by teachers. Without them, the sky is the limit… or perhaps the stars?

# [Fun With Functions (11th–12th Grade)](https://drive.google.com/drive/folders/16Bz6coMwbUoDRcGf0YDKx-LD3TGtUxeT?usp=sharing)

This multipart series was an introduction to functions through the lens of Information and Coding Theory, given to an 11th- and 12th-grade class as an experiment in norm-setting and reflection, and in how those might inform my teaching practice. Dr. Sainza Fernandez was an amazing mentor, supporting my focus on what are often considered ancillary, less content-focused goals. I had not previously produced material on the practice of writing reflections, or on guiding learners in how to actively play with the types of problems posed (more open-ended, exploratory questions). The results were mixed. It turns out a few months of me talking at them and getting discussions going did not undo years of conditioning by the German school system toward closed problems and "right/wrong" binaries. There were just too many changes all at once, though some students did really latch on to these differences in approach. Learners produced some truly inspired coding schemes and seemed to digest the concepts well, but these days I would treat the material quite differently: fewer words, more hands-on experience, and student-led and student-defined problems.

# [Evaluation of the Impact of Comics on Exam Performance: Link to Exam](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1i0CFLTwQjzXxcDtNhcS9xcjyuB5SinQK/view?usp=share_link)

Interspersed with Far Side comics (provided here without them, to avoid a copyright issue), this exam served as part of my masters research project at the Freie Universität Berlin, administered at the Nelson Mandela School to two (lucky?) 10th-grade math classes. The research results were inconclusive, as they were based on too small a sample size, but the project was fun, and it was the first time most of the kids had ever chuckled during a math test. Creating a fun and challenging standardized test was a blast, though of course the timing and the differences from their normal exam structure surely influenced the results. Caveats aside, I think multiple choice gets a bad reputation because most such tests are not particularly fun, funny or challenging. Fixing those three things yields significant benefits for the learner (and the teacher)!

# [Socio-Emotional Learning in STEM Contexts: Link to Workshops](https://jsaaron.carrd.co/#)

(Pending) These handouts were produced as part of a series of workshops given to STEM and Humanities tutors while I was working at Paper.co, under a great manager and informed by a great head of teaching and learning. I learned a great deal giving these workshops: that we, as teachers, are often informed and shaped by our own educational experiences in ways that are difficult to notice, assess, and (should we find them unhelpful) change. As I now often work in adult learning contexts, I find that focusing on this very aspect, the habits and expectations an adult learner brings with them from their schooling, is often more important than access to quality teachers or content.
