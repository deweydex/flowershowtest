# Overly Heady (Post)Introduction:

AI tools are often framed as replacing various aspects of the Learning → Teaching → Creating → Adapting cycle. For our purposes I want to highlight these tools in terms of how I relate to them as a teacher, how I see my students relate to them, and how I see management push or pull in directions around them at various levels. In other words, these are meant to be material that has an opinion: very little of these texts could be described as “neutral.” Instead, they hopefully provide a frame through which to look at a rapidly changing technological environment, giving the reader tools that will help them navigate that environment and maintain a strong grasp of what is important, what is static, and what is dynamic. These highfalutin words lead us to some pretty basic, everyday workshops. As it currently stands there are 4 of 6 workshops available for your perusal, each designed to function relatively independently but also with a relatively clear progression.

These handouts were created to accomplish a few goals, all in the context of a school containing four groups:

1. Students: these folks tend to be important for schools)

1. Teachers: these folks are also important, though that fact is contested by the first group

1. Support Staff: these folks are also important, invisible to the first group, and how appreciated they are by the second group depends on the specific school—at my school they are well loved and appreciated I would say.

1. Management/Leadership: these folks are allegedly important though how much is contested by all three groups in different ways and directions. Since these workshops have yet to be shut down by the management team at my school: I can only say that my school’s management team is both very important and fantastic.

1. Everyone else who is not on this list: these folks might also be important but I promised four groups... I am told they are the ones who pay the bills?

Why start with a funny section on who is involved in a school? As stated, this is about framing: the technological underpinnings of tools like LLMs, RAG models, and Hybrid Search/Generation models are mostly separate from the interpretive (hermeneutical) framework that we use to evaluate them. As the underlying technology changes, we rarely have clarity about how our stance toward the output of these tools should change. Thus my approach is based more in the language of literacy and interpretation, rather than technical understanding: put simply, we need to develop ways of interpreting text generated by these tools, as well as interpreting the *use* of these tools by our students (as well as the other groups above). To me, this is about literacy, and so the first handouts are all centered around a common set of understandings and distinctions, before moving to understand how we, and how our students might use these tools. Thank you for reading a perhaps overly abstract introduction, I hope the material proves more practical and down to earth.
